# Just Diagnosed: Your First 90 Days

Getting a diagnosis can bring relief ("it's not in my head") and overwhelm ("what now?") at the same time. This page walks you through the first 90 days - not everything at once, just the next right step.

## Step 1: Get your records

Your medical records belong to you. Request:

- **Surgical report** - what the surgeon found and what they did (excision, ablation, biopsy)
- **Pathology report** - confirms tissue was actually Endometriosis (histological confirmation)
- **Imaging** - MRI or ultrasound reports showing findings
- **Photos** - many surgeons take intraoperative photos. Ask for copies

Keep these in a folder (physical or digital). You'll need them for second opinions and future specialists.

## Step 2: Understand your diagnosis

Two critical distinctions:

**Excision vs. ablation:**

| Method | What it does | Recurrence |
|---|---|---|
| **Excision** | Cuts out lesions completely, including root | Lower recurrence rate |
| **Ablation** | Burns the surface of lesions | Higher recurrence - roots often remain |

If your surgery used ablation, the lesions may not be fully removed. This doesn't mean you need immediate re-surgery, but it's important context for your ongoing care.

**Staging:**

Your report may mention a stage (I-IV). Important: **stage does not equal pain level**. Stage I can cause severe pain. Stage IV can be found incidentally. The stage describes the extent of visible disease, not your experience. See [Endometriosis staging](/endometriosis/#endometriosis-symptoms-by-stage) for details.

## Step 3: Find an excision specialist

Not all gynecologists are trained in Endometriosis excision. If you need further treatment or a second opinion:

- Use vetted directories like [iCareBetter](https://icarebetter.com/) or [The Yellow Hub](https://www.theyellowhub.org/)
- Ask about excision training, case volume, and multidisciplinary approach
- See our full guide: [Find a Specialist](/find-a-specialist/)

## Step 4: Start tracking symptoms

A written symptom history is the most useful thing you can bring to any appointment. Track:

- Pain levels and locations (daily)
- Period timing and flow
- Bowel and bladder symptoms
- Fatigue and mood
- Medication use and effect

Use our [Symptom Tracker](/tracker/) - all data stays on your device, nothing is transmitted.

## Step 5: Address mental health

A chronic pain diagnosis changes how you see your body and your future. This is normal:

- **Grief** - for the time lost to misdiagnosis, for the life you expected
- **Anger** - at doctors who dismissed you, at the healthcare system
- **Relief** - finally having a name for what you've been experiencing
- **Anxiety** - about fertility, progression, treatment decisions

Consider:

- A therapist experienced with chronic illness (not just general therapy)
- Support communities: [r/Endometriosis](https://www.reddit.com/r/Endometriosis/), [The Mighty](https://themighty.com/topic/endometriosis/)
- Full resources: [Mental Health](/mental-health/)

## Step 6: Plan financially

Endometriosis treatment costs can be substantial. Start by understanding the landscape:

- **Average surgery costs** range from $2,000 to $50,000+ depending on complexity and location
- **Insurance** may not cover out-of-network specialists. Learn about the No Surprises Act
- **Financial assistance** exists through organizations in multiple countries

See real patient-reported costs and financial planning tips: [Surgery Costs](/surgery-costs/)

## What NOT to do right away

- **Don't panic-research at 2am** - the internet has a lot of worst-case stories. This guide gives you the sequence
- **Don't rush into treatment decisions** - unless you're in acute danger, you have time to find the right specialist
- **Don't compare your experience** - stage, symptoms, and treatment response vary enormously between patients
- **Don't try to do everything at once** - follow the steps above over weeks, not days
